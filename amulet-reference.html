How to read this catalogue

Every claim carries its warrant

A charm's catalogue facts and its purported powers are different kinds of statement. This reference keeps them visibly apart, so the tradition is represented faithfully rather than flattened into either scepticism or sales copy. Each entry is tagged by how its claims are known.

Catalogue

Recorded fact — a name, material, temple, maker, date, or a listing that exists in the corpus (manuscript metadata, marketplace data, a dated piece). Verifiable.

Tradition

What practitioners hold to be true — the domain of power a charm carries and the conditions of its use. Reported faithfully as belief, neither endorsed nor debunked.

Inference

Reasoned context drawn from general knowledge to connect or explain entries. Plausible, but not tied to a specific corpus source — read with that caution.

Domains of power appear as chips on each entry: ProtectionMetta / CharmWealthHealingAuthority — the Thai practitioner's own axes (คงกระพัน · เมตตามหานิยม · โชคลาภ · สุขภาพ · อำนาจบารมี), not outside categories imposed on the tradition.

The provenance spine

The seam is time, not authenticity

There is no line where the "real" tradition ends and the "commercial" one begins. A votive tablet pressed from Hariphunchai clay and a garuda pendant sold today belong to a single, continuous practice of carrying power on the body.

c. 11th–13th c.Hariphunchai · Lanna roots

Votive clay & the palm-leaf treatise

The oldest surviving northern charms are kiln-fired clay tablets — พระรอด and the Lamphun votive set — pressed as merit and buried in stupas. Alongside them, monks copy yant diagrams and katha onto palm leaf in Khom script. Inference the practice of inscribing sacred syllables predates any single surviving object.

1860s–1930sThe named-monk era

The consecrating masters

Amulets become signed works. Somdej Toh's พระสมเด็จ (1860s) and, in the north, Khruba Srivichai (1878–1939) — the Lanna saint whose followers built the Doi Suthep road in 1935 — attach a charm's power to a specific hand and a specific temple. Provenance becomes value. Catalogue

2006–2016The craze decades

Mass devotion, mass markets

The จตุคามรามเทพ wealth medallion peaks in 2006–07 — an estimated ฿22 billion trade, ending in a fatal crowd-crush. A decade later, ensouled ลูกเทพ "child-angel" dolls have their own boom and fade. Catalogue The charm becomes a financial and media event.

NowMutelu · the phone screen

The listing as living practice

Gen-Z reframes the whole field as มูเตลู (mutelu) — an everyday, unembarrassed engagement with charms, shrines and luck. Sellers state a piece's พุทธคุณ (efficacy) in the listing itself. Chinese ปี่เซียะ and Japanese โอมาโมริ sit beside temple amulets. The market is the tradition, continued. Tradition

What the market adds

Seven more varieties, each a different kind of thing to carry — a viral modern deity, a guardian giant, a natural anomaly, a monk's own bodily relic, a topical potion, a living plant. Not appended for volume: each is drawn straight from what the market actually stocks and what its own listings claim, so the catalogue's shape matches the tradition's present shape, not just its past.

F·16 · the phenomenon of the decade
ไอ้ไข่Ai Khaithe boy-spirit of Wat Chedi

F·20 · the bodily relic
ชานหมากChan Maka monk's own chewed betel
Form preserved quid, sometimes lacquered

Not made for the wearer at all — a revered monk's own chewed betel-nut quid, kept rather than discarded. Closer to a saint's relic than a manufactured charm: the power is his own body's residue, not an inscription. Tradition carries the monk's own barami directly, undiluted by any maker's craft. Catalogue by far the highest average price of any market category surveyed — ฿30–15,500, avg ฿785 — pieces from named masters (Luang Pu See, Luang Pho Thob) command the top of that range.

F·22 · the living charge
ว่านWaana charged tuber or plant
Form living plant · dried root · ground powder

Not an object at all but a category of plant — certain tubers (e.g. ว่านดอกทอง, "golden-flower waan") are grown and kept for an innate charge, then used raw, steeped into oil, or ground into a charm's filling. Hundreds of kinds, not a handful

The specimen catalogue above is a reader's selection — real breadth needed a real count. This taxonomy comes from screening the market's own listing text against a broad lexicon of known Thai amulet terms, not a small curated seed-list: 112 distinct varieties survived the check, after discarding substring false-positives (Thai has no word-spacing, so short terms like "crab" or "bird" collide with unrelated words) and non-amulet noise (Halloween decor, fishing lures) that a blind keyword count would have swept in. Catalogue. n is listing-hits within the term; a title can carry more than one term.

Yant · the diagram beneath the charm

What is actually inscribed

Nearly every charm above carries, somewhere, a yant — a sacred diagram of geometry, deity-syllables and katha. The takrut rolls one up; the sak yant poks one into skin; the ผ้ายันต์ prints one on cloth. The grammar is shared.

The formulary · ตำรา · newly opened

How the charm is actually made

The treatise ตำราสร้างเครื่องรางของขลัง — 170 pages, once locked in scanned images — has now been read by OCR. It is a working manual: ~60 formulas, each opening with the invocation สิทธิการิยะ ("may it be accomplished"), then giving the material, the letters to inscribe, the katha to chant, the count of recitations, the use, and the taboos. A sample is transcribed below. Catalogue on the recipes; Inference on the English reading of the katha; Tradition on the powers claimed.

First, the grammar: ลงอุณาโลม

Every inscribed charm is finished with the unalome — the reversed Khom letter อุ, drawn as a rising crown-stroke that stands for the sage whose mind runs straight to the summit and does not scatter. The treatise sets the stroke to a syllable-ladder: each notch (หยัก) of the stroke is "charged" with a fixed na before the line is drawn straight up. This is the shared grammar beneath the takrut, the tattoo, and the cloth yant.

The charm is not inert — it is fed

The clearest thing the formulary reveals is that many of these objects are treated as alive. The Kuman Thong takes rice daily; the Wua Thanu takes grass and water; the หุ่นพยนต์ effigy is stuffed with mercury and fed rice, pork and liquor. A charm is not bought once and forgotten — it is roused, named, fed and told its purpose, and it lapses if neglected. The relationship, not the object, is the technology.

Inference These are transcribed as documentation of a living tradition, not as instructions to follow — the Kuman Thong's historical form in particular is recorded here ethnographically; today's practice uses consecrated effigies, not human remains. Inference The market absorbs freely: Chinese ปี่เซียะ (Pixiu) and Japanese โอมาโมริ (omamori) are catalogued and sold alongside temple amulets without friction. A charm is judged by the power it carries, not the border it came from.

The deepest seam, now opened. The 170-page treatise ตำราสร้างเครื่องรางของขลัง ("Treatise on Making Sacred Charms & Amulets") once existed in the corpus only as scanned page images — its recipes locked. It has now been read by OCR, and the formulary above is drawn straight from its pages: real materials, real katha, real rites. The ledger is one seam deeper than it was.

It is now fully transcribed and translated — all 170 pages, page by page, re-read at high resolution: 160 formulas, 435 katha, Thai beside English, from the Khom-script primer that opens the book to the ceremonial fan that closes it. The complete index sits below; the seven recipes above are a reader's selection. The bilingual text is loaded into the manuscript corpus as a first-class volume — a page-by-page reader (Thai + English) keyed to catalogue manuscript 6983. What remains is a scholar's proofing of the Pali-Khmer katha, whose orthography the book itself spells inconsistently. Catalogue on what the treatise records; Inference on the reading of its incantations.
